Interpreting the Eerie: A Synthetic Reading of the Face in the Dark

A computational and critical reading of Ruskin Bond’s short story: how fear is built from words, and where the colonial past is still audible.

Move your cursor or finger across the page to hold up a torch.

What is Synthetic Reading?

Synthetic reading is a method that combines a human reader’s judgement with machine-assisted analysis. The researcher first reads only a sample of a text, forms an initial impression, and frames a research problem. A language model then extracts patterns from the whole text, and the researcher checks, visualises and interprets them using literary and cultural theory.

It is “synthetic” because it joins two ways of reading: the close, partial, reflexive reading of a person and the exhaustive, systematic scanning of a computer.

The aim of this project

To examine how Ruskin Bond’s “The Face in the Dark” builds fear through vivid imagery, and to trace which words in the story belong to a postcolonial vocabulary. The project also tests whether a synthetic reading can support, rather than replace, a critical interpretation of a short supernatural story.

Research question

How does the author employ vivid imagery of fear in “The Face in the Dark”, and which words from a postcolonial vocabulary appear in it?

Procedure

  1. Partial reading

    The story was split into sentences, giving 43. About 20 percent, 9 sentences, were selected at random. The sample happens to include both the first and the last sentence, so the story’s arc can be followed through it.

  2. Initial impression

    The opening sentences establish Mr Oliver as an Anglo-Indian teacher, an identity that matters throughout. The sample suggests a supernatural horror story with a dense use of imagery and an open ending that invites reader-response readings.

  3. Uploading the text

    The complete story was supplied to the language model without pre-processing.

  4. Prompting the model

    A single prompt asked for two exhaustive extractions: fear imagery and postcolonial aspects.

    Read the prompt

    To operationalize the analysis of the imagery of fear in the story, extract from the entire textual corpus all lexical items and multi-word expressions that function semantically within the theme of fear. Make another extraction including the post-colonial aspects in the story. The extraction should be exhaustive rather than selective, span the full length of the text without privileging any single section, and preserve the original lexical form of each item. The output must be structured in JSON format, enabling transparent inspection, reproducibility, and computational analysis.

  5. Extraction

    The model returned two JSON files, fear.json and postcolonial.json, listing each lexical item with its category, expression type, lines and a short interpretation.

  6. Visualisation

    Python (pandas, matplotlib and seaborn) matched every extracted expression against each sentence and plotted the distribution of fear and postcolonial vocabulary across the story, by number of occurrences and by thematic density.

  7. Interpretation

    The charts were read against the story and against literary theory, as set out below.

Visualisations

Each chart plots the extracted vocabulary against sentence position.

Interpretation

Fear. The words of fear are spread through the story rather than gathered at one point. This steady deployment keeps the reader in an eerie atmosphere from the sad sounds of the pines to the faceless boy, so the horror comes from accumulated imagery as much as from the final reveal.

Postcolonial vocabulary. The postcolonial aspect stays present until the end of the story, where the watchman’s “sahib” is heard once more before the lamp goes out and the scene is dark again. The vocabulary of an English-style school, an Anglo-Indian teacher and a polite title of address keeps the colonial hierarchy in view even as the supernatural takes over.

Open ending. The story stops at the moment of darkness and leaves the reader to complete its meaning, which makes it a useful case for reader-response theory: the final fear is produced in the reader’s imagination.

Limitations. This is a single short text. The extraction depends on one prompt and on the model used, and the charts count words rather than measure effect, so the findings are interpretive evidence, not proof.
